\[

**3. Part 1: use a hill climber that can exchange models**

I recommend a **multi-start first-improvement hill climber** with:

- Single-bit flips.
- One-for-one exchanges: remove one selected model and add one unselected model.
- Rejection of infeasible neighbours.
- Acceptance only of strictly improving feasible neighbours.
- Restart after exhausting the neighbourhood without improvement.

The exchange operator is important here. **All utilities in your datasets are positive.** A strict hill climber using only one-bit moves can accept additions, but removing any selected model reduces utility. Once the available capacity is filled, it cannot remove a poor choice to accommodate a better one.

An exchange makes that replacement possible in one move.

Construct each starting solution by shuffling model indices and adding models whenever they fit. Shuffle the neighbourhood before searching it, accepting the first improvement. Keep the best solution across restarts.

**5. Make repair strategy your controlled experimental difference**

Your larger instance has a useful asymmetry:

| Resource | Capacity divided by total demand |
|---|---:|
| 1 | 63.4% |
| 2 | **32.7%** |
| 3 | 85.1% |

Resource 2 also reaches its full capacity in the reference optimum. This motivates a meaningful comparison between a general resource-density rule and a rule that responds to current overload.

**Variant A: static normalised-density repair**

Define:

\[
a_{ij}=\frac{r_{ij}}{b_i},
\qquad
q_j=\frac{p_j}{\sum_i a_{ij}}.
\]

While infeasible, remove the selected model with the lowest \(q_j\).

This favours utility per unit of aggregate normalised resource consumption. Its limitation is that it charges consumption of every resource, including resources with plenty of spare capacity.

**Variant B: overload-aware repair**

Calculate current normalised excess:

\[
e_i=\max\left(0,\frac{u_i-b_i}{b_i}\right).
\]

For each selected model, calculate the violation relief its removal would provide:

\[
h_j=\sum_i\min(e_i,a_{ij}).
\]

Remove the model maximising:

\[
\frac{h_j}{p_j}.
\]

Then recompute the excess and repeat until feasible.

This targets **current violation reduction per utility sacrificed**. The minimum prevents crediting resource relief beyond the actual overload.

For both variants, finish with the same refill procedure: consider unselected models in descending static-density order and accept additions that fit.

That keeps the experimental treatment narrow: **only the deletion priority changes**.

Describe the overload-aware rule as your engineering adaptation. Do not claim it reproduces a published algorithm or guarantees better results.
